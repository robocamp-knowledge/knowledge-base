Testing the new LEGO® Education Computer Science & AI set Part #2: Which of these robots looks like it was designed for 11-year-olds?  After preparing and testing almost 30 different constructions with LEGO® Education Computer Science & AI, one concern keeps returning: its problem may not be what the set can do, but how students perceive it.  The set certainly has educational potential. Some models use interesting mechanisms, and some support meaningful coding activities. Several aspects of its actual capabilities remain open to debate.  But the problem of perception is becoming increasingly difficult to ignore.  Look at the constructions in the images and compare similar projects created with the two LEGO Education systems.  SPIKE Prime usually communicates:  ⚙️ mechanisms 🤖 robotics 🔧 engineering 📏 technical problem-solving  The new Computer Science & AI system often communicates something different:  🎨 soft, pastel forms 🧩 a more playful visual language 🟦 large, rounded electronic components 🧸 an aesthetic closer to products for younger learners  This does not mean that these constructions are bad, or that pastel colors cannot be used with older students. The problem is the overall perception.  At the age of 11 or 12, students are already highly sensitive to whether an activity feels appropriate for their age. A project can be educationally valuable and still be rejected because it looks as though it was designed for much younger children.  Until now, LEGO Education product lines for different age groups differed not only in their building complexity and selection of elements. Their electronics also had a different visual identity. A system for younger learners looked different from a robotics platform for older students. The product itself communicated progression: this is the next level.  That progression is much less visible in the new LEGO Education ecosystem.  The electronic components used across solutions for 5+, 8+ and 11+ learners are visually identical. The same white housings, rounded forms and friendly visual language appear at every age level.  So where is the visual progression that tells an eleven-year-old:  “This was designed for someone my age”?  LEGO has traditionally been very good at aspirational design. Its products often make children feel slightly older, more capable and closer to working with real technology. Here, the opposite may be happening.  The name Computer Science & AI promises a mature, future-focused learning experience, but many of the models visually communicate a younger and more playful category than the declared 11+ positioning.  After building almost 30 models, we increasingly believe that this perception problem may be more serious than the hardware limitations themselves. Hardware capabilities can sometimes be extended through better projects and better lesson design.  But if students look at the system and immediately think, “This is for younger children,” that is much harder to repair.  Without looking at the product names or age labels, which of these constructions would you identify as the one designed for students aged 11+?  #RoboCamp #LEGOEducation #EducationalRobotics #STEMEducation #EdTech #ComputerScienceEducation #SPIKEPrime #LEGOEducationAI #RoboticsEducation

Testing the new LEGO Education Computer Science & AI set Part #1: 45-minute lessons  45-minute robotics lessons are not a new idea.  But they are becoming more and more important in real school timetables.  LEGO Education is now clearly positioning the new Computer Science & AI set around short, 45-minute classroom lessons — and honestly, we think this is the right direction.  ⏱️ Teachers need robotics activities that can fit into a regular school day.  But here is the key point:  A short lesson is not created by the box.  It is created by the lesson plan.  That is why we have been developing short SPIKE Prime lessons for some time already.  For example, in our SPIKE Prime Forklift lesson, students work with mechanics, movement logic and structured programming — all within a compact classroom format.  Now we are testing a similar idea with the new LEGO Education Computer Science & AI set.  Can you run a 45-minute lesson with the new set?  Yes.  But can you run several 45-minute lessons smoothly, one after another, during a normal school day?  That is a different question.  🔋 With SPIKE Prime, this part of the classroom workflow was simpler: one hub, one battery, one charging routine.  With the new Computer Science & AI electronics, each wireless component has its own battery. This opens up some interesting design possibilities, but it also means charging has to be planned before the lesson — it is not something teachers can always easily fix once the lesson has started.  So this is not really a question of whether the new set is “bad”.  It is not.  It is a question of what schools actually need.  If your goal is to run better 45-minute robotics lessons, you do not automatically need a new set.  You need a better lesson plan.  #RoboCamp #LEGOEducation #EducationalRobotics #STEMEducation #EdTech #CodingForKids #SPIKEPrime #ComputerScience #AIinEducation #HandsOnLearning

The recent announcement of LEGO Education’s Computer Science & AI sets raises an important question: should AI in robotics education focus on using AI tools, or on understanding how learning algorithms actually work?  Understanding how AI learns — through feedback, adaptation, and iteration — can already be taught today, even with robotics sets that many schools already have in their classrooms.  One example is Launcher AI+, an advanced SPIKE Prime activity developed by the RoboCamp team.  Students build a catapult-style robot that repeatedly launches a ball toward a target. After each attempt, the robot receives feedback — the shot was too short or too far — and adjusts its launch power accordingly. With every iteration, the program updates its parameters and gradually improves accuracy.  There is no black-box AI here. Students design the learning logic themselves and can clearly see how data and feedback shape the robot’s behavior over time.  This is what learning about AI can look like — without new hardware, and without using AI as a shortcut instead of understanding how it works.
